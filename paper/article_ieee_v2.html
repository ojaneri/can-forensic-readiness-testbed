Abstract—Modern vehicles rely on distributed electronic control units connected through in-vehicle networks such as the Controller Area Network (CAN), whose legacy design lacks native authentication and message-origin verification. However, realistic automotive cybersecurity experimentation remains difficult for classrooms, small laboratories, and forensic practitioners because vehicles, documented ECUs, and commercial hardware-in-the-loop platforms can be costly or unsafe to operate. This paper presents a software-defined, low-cost CAN testbed for automotive cybersecurity education and forensic-readiness analysis. The artifact combines virtual CAN traffic generation, controlled spoofing and flooding scenarios, normalized datasets, evidence manifests, SHA-256 hash preservation, and a client-side observer demo. Two software-only experiments generated a combined dataset of 42,288 labeled CAN frames, including a 10-minute run with 40,200 frames. A rule-based triage heuristic is used only as a label-consistency and forensic-screening sanity check, not as a real-vehicle intrusion detection benchmark. The revised contribution is therefore an evidence-preserving educational workflow rather than a claim of physical CAN fidelity or operational IDS performance.

Index Terms—automotive cybersecurity, CAN bus, forensic readiness, testbed, virtual CAN, reproducibility, cybersecurity education.

I. Introduction

CAN remains widely deployed in production and legacy vehicles. Its design prioritizes robustness, timing, and low cost, but does not provide native authentication, confidentiality, or strong sender verification. Once an adversary or analyst reaches a CAN segment, receivers typically process frames according to identifiers and payload semantics rather than authenticated origin.

Automotive cybersecurity research has produced physical benches, HIL platforms, public datasets, and intrusion detection systems. Yet these assets are not equally accessible to resource-constrained education and forensic environments. Real vehicles can be expensive and unsafe to manipulate; used ECUs may require proprietary pinouts, immobilizer pairing, and careful bench powering; commercial HIL systems may exceed classroom budgets.

This paper addresses the early-stage training and forensic-readiness gap by presenting a software-defined CAN testbed. The goal is not to reproduce all physical properties of CAN or to benchmark operational IDS performance. Instead, the goal is to provide a reproducible, safe, evidence-preserving workflow for teaching CAN attacks, generating labeled traces, preserving artifacts, and performing preliminary triage.

The contributions are: (i) a low-cost software-defined CAN testbed architecture; (ii) controlled baseline, spoofing, flooding, and recovery scenarios; (iii) an evidence workflow with input plans, raw logs, normalized CSV files, manifests, and SHA-256 hashes; (iv) an English dataset package with 42,288 labeled frames; and (v) an observer-safe client-side demo plus a rule-based triage sanity check.

II. Background and Related Work

Classic automotive security studies demonstrated that in-vehicle networks can be manipulated once attackers obtain access to relevant interfaces [1]–[3]. Subsequent work surveyed CAN security challenges, including replay, message fabrication, bus availability, and proprietary signal interpretation [4], [5]. Automotive standards such as ISO/SAE 21434 and UNECE R155 provide cybersecurity engineering and management context [15], [16].

Testbeds range from software simulators to benches with commercial off-the-shelf ECUs. ICSim provides a visual CAN training environment; Caring Caribou supports CAN and diagnostic exploration; VitroBench and CANBench represent more physical bench-oriented approaches [6]–[9]. Public datasets such as ROAD and can-train-and-test support IDS research and provide realistic traces, but they are not designed as full forensic-readiness workflows [10]–[12].

III. Testbed and Evidence Workflow

A. Architecture

The bus layer uses the python-can virtual backend and can be migrated to SocketCAN/vcan. Simulated ECUs generate periodic engine/cluster, body, display, and background frames. Attack modules inject fabricated state or randomized high-cardinality traffic. The logger stores raw annotated CAN-style logs and normalized CSV files.

C. Safety Boundary

All reported experiments are authorized and software-only. No vehicle, ECU, CAN adapter, external network, or operational system was targeted. The observer demo replays stored artifacts and does not execute live attacks.

IV. Experimental Methodology

Experiments contain four phases: baseline, spoofing, flooding, and recovery. Baseline and recovery generate normal periodic traffic. Spoofing fabricates didactic vehicle state on ID 0x100. Flooding injects randomized identifiers and payloads to create high-cardinality noise.

V. Results

The most frequent IDs in the combined dataset are 0x100, 0x188, 0x120, and 0x300, representing stable simulated traffic. Flooding adds many low-count randomized IDs, increasing diversity. Payload entropy is approximately 2.94 bits during baseline/recovery and 7.998 bits during flooding. Inter-arrival metrics were recomputed separately and are not used as primary evidence because cross-experiment aggregation can introduce discontinuities.

In Experiment 006, speed on ID 0x100 ranged from 27 to 92 km/h during baseline, with mean 61.04 km/h. Spoofing produced 180–200 km/h values, mean 195.00 km/h. Recovery returned to a normal range with mean 61.56 km/h.

VI. Triage Sanity Check

A rule-based heuristic was evaluated only as a forensic triage sanity check. It is not presented as an IDS benchmark. The rules classify flooding using high ID cardinality or unknown-ID ratio, spoofing using decoded speed above 130 km/h on ID 0x100, and other windows as normal. Because these rules align with the didactic generation process, high performance indicates label consistency rather than real-world robustness.

Across 615 windows, the heuristic achieved 0.9984 accuracy and 0.9982 macro-F1. The confusion matrix was: normal 368/369 correctly classified, spoofing 123/123, and flooding 123/123. One normal window was flagged as flooding.

VII. Discussion

The artifact is strongest as a reproducible educational and forensic-readiness package. A reader can inspect the attack plan, regenerate or replay traces, validate hashes, read a data dictionary, and inspect a safe client-side demo. This combination differentiates the work from a simple simulator or detector script.

The main trade-off is fidelity. Physical benches and real datasets provide stronger realism, while the proposed testbed provides lower cost, safety, and transparent semantics. The intended path is incremental: software-only learning first, then SocketCAN/vcan, ICSim/Caring Caribou integration, and finally hardware-in-the-loop with used clusters or legacy ECUs.

VIII. Limitations

The dataset is synthetic and generated by the same framework that defines labels. It does not model physical CAN arbitration, electrical noise, transceiver errors, termination, bus-off behavior, gateway filtering, ECU timing constraints, or OEM signal accuracy. The rule-based heuristic is deliberately simple and should not be treated as operational IDS evidence. IX. Conclusion

This paper presented a software-defined low-cost CAN testbed for automotive cybersecurity education and forensic-readiness analysis. The artifact generates labeled CAN traffic, preserves evidence using manifests and hashes, provides normalized datasets and analysis scripts, and supports observer-safe replay. The revised framing avoids overclaiming detection or physical realism and emphasizes reproducible, low-cost, evidence-aware learning. Future work will add external traffic sources such as ICSim or public dataset replay and then progress toward SocketCAN hardware and legacy ECU benches.
